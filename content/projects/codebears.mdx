CodeBears is a summer computing camp at Morgan State University. I coordinated
it across three summers, which means the version of it I know best is the
operational one: recruiting, scheduling, matching mentors to groups, and the
daily work of keeping a room of middle and high school students engaged with
material that many of them arrived believing was not for them.

The research question underneath the logistics was about stereotypes. Not
whether students could learn to program, which was never in doubt, but what
they believed about who programs and who belongs in computing. Two research
projects came out of the camp: one presented at SIGCSE 2024 examining
stereotypes and their relationship to the STEM pipeline, and another presented
at BICE 2024 exploring what the camp revealed about empowering underrepresented
youth in computing.

## What the camp taught me about teaching

Most of what I now believe about teaching came from this room rather than from
a course. I often saw students disengage not because the material was beyond
their ability, but because confusion could become evidence that they did not
belong. That observation has shaped how I think about computing education:
difficulty should be treated as part of learning, not as a signal that someone
does not belong.

Everything I do in a classroom now is downstream of trying to break that
inference.

That idea also follows directly into my current work with SensDS. CodeBears
taught me to pay attention to the moments when students decide that something
is not for them. With SensDS, I am interested in a related problem at a
different level: what happens when we stop hiding the difficult parts of
machine learning and instead make them visible and understandable? Rather than
giving students a polished dataset and a model that appears to simply work or
fail, SensDS lets them collect data, make decisions about that data, train a
model, and encounter problems like distribution shift firsthand. In both cases,
the goal is the same: make the hard parts of computing something students can
engage with rather than something that quietly tells them they do not belong.

The associated poster and presentation received third place in the ACM
