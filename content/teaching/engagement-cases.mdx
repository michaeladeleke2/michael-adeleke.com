## What the activity asked

Read eight short cases describing students who present differently in class, and for each one answer three questions: is this an engagement problem, what might be contributing to it, and what would you do Monday morning if this student were in your class.

## What I made

Responses to the first four cases: the bored expert, the defeated novice, the grade optimizer, and the silent student. For the bored expert I proposed harder bonus problems and letting the student teach part of a session. For the grade optimizer I proposed tying concepts to students' experience outside the course.

## What I learned

Two of my four answers are wrong by the course's own definitions, and seeing them beside each other showed me why. For the defeated novice I answered that this is not an engagement problem but a motivation problem, yet motivation is one of the two strands of engagement in the double helix model, so a student who says they are just not a CS person is an engagement problem of exactly the affective kind. For the silent student I answered yes and proposed more group discussion to get them talking, even though the case states that the student consistently produces excellent work and demonstrates deep understanding, and even though my own syllabus says participation does not require speaking every class. I wrote a policy protecting that student in one document and prescribed the opposite in another. The pattern in both wrong answers is that I used visible behaviour as the measure of engagement, which is precisely the distinction the course draws between level of mental activity and observable participation. My answers to the bored expert and the grade optimizer hold up better, because in those cases the behaviour and the underlying problem happened to point the same way.

## How this shapes my teaching

The first habit to build is separating "this student is disengaged" from "this student is quiet", and checking their work before acting on the silence. The silent student case is the test of that, and I failed it. For the defeated novice, my answer was office hours and extra practice, which adds work to a student who already feels behind; the course's approach is to subdivide tasks with individual deadlines, set reachable goals, and use a study buddy policy, which lowers the bar to re entry instead of raising it. In CS 101 that means building a check on understanding that does not run through speaking: a written exit response, a code submission, or a one on one during the pair activity.
