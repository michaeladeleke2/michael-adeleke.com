## What the activity asked

Produce a 50 minute class session plan for CS 101 on debugging Python programs, including a learning goal, student learning outcomes, materials, a minute by minute activity sequence with the purpose of each block, a table of assessments and knowledge checks, sample questions, UDL connections, and instructor notes.

## What I made

Nine blocks across the fifty minutes, moving from a written retrieval warm up, through a mini lecture on syntax, runtime and logic errors, into twelve minutes of collaborative debugging in pairs, and out through a debrief and an individual exit question. Four outcomes anchor the plan, the fourth being that students explain why a proposed correction fixes a problem.

## What I learned

Drawing the checkpoints against the clock showed me something I missed while writing the plan. I have no evidence about any particular student until four minutes remain, and nothing I can do with it when it arrives. My own instructor note says that if many students miss a Kahoot question I should pause and address the misconception, but every one of the fifty minutes is already committed, so the plan instructs me to do something it leaves no room for. The twelve minute collaborative block is the one that will overrun: two to three programs in twelve minutes is four to six minutes each including explanation, correction and testing, which is optimistic for novices. The strongest item in the plan is a Kahoot question I wrote almost offhand, asking what the concern is when you change three lines of code and the program starts working, because it targets the behaviour novices actually have rather than the definitions they can memorize.

## How this shapes my teaching

I would cut the debrief from six minutes to four and drop one Kahoot question, which buys back the slack my own instructor note assumes exists. I would collect the individual responses from the second knowledge check rather than only watching students compare with a partner, which moves my first individual evidence from minute 46 to minute 23. I would plan two broken programs and treat a third as optional, rather than writing two to three and discovering mid class that it was three. The requirement that students explain why a fix works is the part I would protect under time pressure, since it is the only thing separating systematic debugging from changing lines until the error disappears. The instructor note about circulating and asking for reasoning instead of giving answers is the hardest thing in the plan to actually do, and I should treat it as a rule rather than as advice.
