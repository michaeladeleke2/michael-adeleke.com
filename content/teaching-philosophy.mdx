I teach computing to students who have often already concluded that computing
is not for them. That conclusion is rarely about ability. It is an inference
students draw from confusion: they hit something hard, they look around, they
decide the difficulty is evidence about themselves rather than evidence about
the material. Most of my teaching is an attempt to break that inference.

Three summers coordinating CodeBears, a K-12 computing camp for
underrepresented youth, is where I learned this rather than read it. The
students who disengaged were almost never the ones who found the work
difficult. They were the ones who had privately decided that being confused
disqualified them. What worked was not making the material easier. It was
making confusion ordinary — narrating my own dead ends, showing the version of
the program that did not run, treating a wrong answer as information about the
problem rather than about the person who gave it.

When I taught Introduction to Computer Science I as adjunct faculty at Morgan
State, this shaped concrete decisions. I designed assessments that could be
attempted more than once, because a single-shot exam teaches students that
their first understanding is their permanent one. I asked students to explain
code they had not written before asking them to write any, because reading is
where misconceptions surface cheaply.

My research pushes in the same direction. I build systems that make invisible
signals legible — radar returns rendered as spectrograms a student can move in
front of and watch change. The pedagogical bet in SensDS is that a student who
collects their own data, watches their own model fail on it, and sees why, has
learned something a clean dataset cannot teach. The novelty detector that
flags out-of-distribution captures started as an engineering feature and
became the most useful teaching moment in the system, because it turns
distribution shift from a lecture term into something that happens to you.

I want students to leave my classroom with the belief that difficulty is
normal, that their own data is worth collecting, and that the tools are
theirs to open up. Those three beliefs do more for persistence in computing
than any amount of content coverage.
